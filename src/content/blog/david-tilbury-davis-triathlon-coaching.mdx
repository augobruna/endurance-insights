Last week on the Human Endurance Podcast, we released an episode with David Tilbury-Davis, one of the most respected and long-serving coaches in triathlon. With a career spanning since 1995, David has coached a roster of the sport’s biggest names—including Sky Monch, Lionel Sanders, and David McNamee—to world titles and national records.

In a conversation that spanned from the fundamentals of endurance to the future of artificial intelligence, David shared his “evidence-led” coaching philosophy. He repeatedly dismantled the idea of a single “panacea” for success, arguing that consistency in the basics is what truly moves the needle.

Here are some of the key takeaways from this incredible episode.

### 1. The 80/15/5 Rule of Performance

David breaks down athletic improvement into a simple framework:

- **80% is the basics:** This isn’t just training. It’s the total lifestyle—sleep, nutrition timing, stress management, and professionalism.
- **15% is proven approaches:** This is where a coach applies known principles and methods that have worked for similar athletes.
- **5% is guesswork:** This is the “secret sauce”—experimenting with new science or novel techniques, but only after the other 95% is locked in.

He notes that too many athletes chase the 5% (like “blood flow restriction”) while neglecting the 80% (like getting 30 fewer minutes of sleep to scroll on social media).

### 2. Imposter Syndrome: The “Fuel for the Bonfire”

Perhaps the most fascinating part of our discussion was on athlete psychology. David estimates that **90-95% of high-performing individuals** suffer from imposter syndrome.

He shared a powerful metaphor:

- For the **most successful athletes**, those insecurities and anxieties become the **“fuel for the bonfire of their success,”** driving them to constantly refine and improve.
- For **other athletes**, those same insecurities become the **“bonfire that consumes their ability to succeed.”**

He also links this to burnout, which often occurs when an athlete’s identity becomes tied to their *outcomes* (”I am a winner”) rather than their *process* (”I am a person who is committed to being my best”).

### 3. AI as a “Force Multiplier,” Not a Replacement

As a coach who spends nearly half his time on research, David has a strong perspective on AI. He sees it as a **“force multiplier”** that will democratize knowledge and handle the complex data analysis that currently bogs down many coaches.

He believes this will create an “identity crisis” for coaches whose primary value is their knowledge of formulas and data. The true value of a coach, he argues, will be forced back to its roots: **behavioral engineering, motivation, communication, and human connection.** As he puts it, “people need people.”

### 4. Are You “Reading Tea Leaves” With Your Data?

We asked David about the hype around metrics like lactate and HRV. His answer was refreshingly direct.

He called lactate training a “fad that comes back every decade” and warned that “dipping into it” without full context is no better than **“reading tea leaves.”** To be useful, it requires either rigorous lab testing or constant measurement to build context.

The same goes for HRV. He had an athlete who stopped measuring it because the stress of waking up and taking it perfectly (before their 4-year-old jumped on their head) was *creating* the very stress it was supposed to measure.

Often, he says, the most powerful tool is simply asking the athlete: “How are you feeling?”

### 5. The Most Common Mistake in Triathlon Training?

His answer was immediate: “Really long runs.”
